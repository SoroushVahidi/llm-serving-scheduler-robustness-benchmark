\begin{abstract}
A shared execution contract can change what a scheduler comparison means:
nominally different policies may have the same executable consequences,
while the remaining differences reward different objectives. We present a
reproducible case study of four LLM-serving scheduler adaptations under
CM-0.1, a synthetic model with fixed batch cost and bounded residency.
The contract yields three observed behaviors: chunked-FCFS and decode-first
produce identical per-request terminal states and metrics in all 1,440 matched conditions.
Their priority lists can differ without changing executed work. SLO-gated's
urgency gate is inactive, but prompt-length admission ordering remains active.
Across six source/capacity aggregates from 120 trace-derived windows,
SLO-gated reduces mean latency by 11.3--34.0\% against chunked-FCFS while
reducing token throughput by 1.65--4.01\%; BurstGPT mean window-p95 latency
increases by 3.72\% and 18.97\%. A saved-record example connects lower
mean latency to earlier completions for many requests alongside a longer
drain and worse p95. No source-mean comparison has a strict 10\% practical
reversal, although window-level and metric-dependent reversals occur.
A separate known-truth diagnostic identifies limitations of historical
inference. The contribution is the explanation and measurement of which
scheduler distinctions survive this contract and how they affect ranking
interpretation, not a new scheduler, general scheduling principle, universal
ranking, physical performance prediction, or statistical guarantee.
\end{abstract}
