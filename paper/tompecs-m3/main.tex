\begin{abstract}
Scheduler rankings can conceal a more basic question: do distinct policies
actually select different work? We study four large language model serving
adaptations under a common execution contract that fixes information,
admission, memory reservations, and batch timing. An admission-and-progress
invariant proves that chunked first-come, first-served and decode-first
select identical per-request work from an empty system, even with binding
work budgets. Their normalized transitions and terminal outcomes coincide;
the order of entries in an execution journal need not. A separate finite-work
certificate rules out activation of the implemented decode-urgency gate
in the evaluated regimes. These results identify which nominal mechanisms
can affect execution before interpreting their performance.
Across 120 production-derived windows, the prompt-ordered adaptation reduces
aggregate mean latency by 11.3--34.0\% relative to chunked first-come,
first-served, alongside throughput losses and, on one source, increased
mean window-p95 latency. Source-mean preferences exhibit no reversals
exceeding the declared 10\% margin in both directions, while window-level
and cross-metric reversals persist. The theoretical results concern fixed
policies under the stated contract; the empirical results describe a finite,
temporally related corpus. Neither the fixed-duration model nor these
comparisons establish hardware performance or universal scheduler rankings.
\end{abstract}
