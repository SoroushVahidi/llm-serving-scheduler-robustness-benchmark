\begin{abstract}
Scheduler comparisons require more than identical inputs: the execution
contract determines which policy distinctions can affect request outcomes.
We examine four LLM-serving adaptations under a common contract. Under the original contract, fixed batch timing
and a residency/work bound make chunked-FCFS and decode-first equivalent;
complete terminal records confirm the equality in all 1,440 matched
conditions, despite possible differences in journal ordering. A finite-work
bound certifies urgency inactivity in both the original and budget-perturbed
regimes; prompt-length admission ordering remains active.
Among the three distinct behaviors, SLO-gated reduces aggregate mean latency
by 11.3--34.0\% against chunked-FCFS while reducing finite-window token
throughput by 1.65--4.01\%; BurstGPT mean window-p95 latency increases by
3.72\% and 18.97\%. Equal-window source means exhibit no strict-10\% practical
reversals in 2,376 comparisons, or 864 after removing duplicate behaviors
and endpoints. Window-level and metric-dependent reversals persist: aggregate
stability does not imply a common preference for every window or objective.
A separate 72-cell budget sensitivity preserves the pair's observed equality
on three input-selected BurstGPT windows, without proving equivalence under
arbitrary binding budgets. These results connect effective scheduling behavior to the
interpretation of its performance comparisons.
The model isolates execution semantics; its rates do not predict a
production GPU or establish a universal scheduler ranking.
\end{abstract}
