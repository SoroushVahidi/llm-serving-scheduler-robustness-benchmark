\begin{abstract}
Scheduler comparisons require more than identical inputs: the execution
contract determines which policy distinctions can affect request outcomes.
We connect those structural consequences to measured performance in a
common-model evaluation of four LLM-serving adaptations. Under the original contract, fixed batch timing
and a residency/work bound make chunked-FCFS and decode-first equivalent;
complete terminal records confirm the equality in all 1,440 matched
conditions, despite possible differences in journal ordering. An inactive
urgency gate leaves prompt-length admission ordering as an active mechanism.
Among the three distinct behaviors, SLO-gated reduces aggregate mean latency
by 11.3--34.0\% against chunked-FCFS while reducing finite-window token
throughput by 1.65--4.01\%; BurstGPT mean window-p95 latency increases by
3.72\% and 18.97\%. Saved request timelines illustrate how these objectives
can disagree. Source means exhibit no strict 10\% practical reversals,
although window-level and metric-dependent reversals occur. A post hoc
analysis removing duplicate behaviors and endpoints preserves that contrast.
A prospective 72-cell budget sensitivity preserves the equivalence on three
input-selected BurstGPT windows despite small changes in executed work.
A separate known-truth diagnostic distinguishes historical arithmetic
reproduction from statistical calibration. The contribution is a verified,
contract-specific explanation of which scheduling differences remain
meaningful and how their rankings depend on the comparison estimand.
The model isolates execution semantics; its rates do not predict a
production GPU or establish a universal scheduler ranking.
\end{abstract}
