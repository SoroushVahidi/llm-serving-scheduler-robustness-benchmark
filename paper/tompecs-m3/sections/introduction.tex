\section{Introduction}
LLM serving must reconcile prompt processing, incremental token generation
and retained key--value (KV) state. Batching and memory-management choices
change the feasible schedule; workload lengths and the chosen service
objective change which schedule is preferable
\citep{yu2022orca,kwon2023vllm,agrawal2024sarathi}. A comparison therefore
needs more than a shared trace and a list of scheduler names. It needs an
execution contract specifying what work a decision causes, how resources
are reserved and what information is available when the decision is made.

This issue arose in our historical LLM Serving Scheduler Portability
(LSSP) comparison. Its saved implementations combined different prefill
progression and KV-enforcement rules. Replaying those implementations
reproduced their outcomes, but did not isolate scheduling under a common
resource model. Moreover, practical ranking changes were filtered through
an inferential procedure whose calibration had not been established.
These are separate problems: harmonizing execution does not validate an
inference procedure, and reproducing its arithmetic does not harmonize
execution.

We address the comparison problem with CM-0.1, a deliberately simple,
fully specified discrete-event contract. Four adaptations share the same
service clock, work budget, reservation rule, admissible actions and
online information. Independent event checks reconstruct execution from
journals rather than trusting the engine's summaries. We then evaluate
the complete frozen corpus, retaining every condition and reporting
absolute metrics before rankings. The analysis describes this corpus;
requests and repeated settings do not become independent observations.

Workload-sensitive serving evaluation is established
\citep{papaioannou2024workload}, as are predictive serving simulators
\citep{vidur2024,llmservingsim2024}. Our contribution is a different,
bounded empirical object: the joint behavior of four identified
adaptations under a fixed contract, with matched-window comparisons,
explicit practical-reversal denominators and an account of which ranking
distinctions the model can express. CM-0.1 uses a fixed batch cost rather
than fitted GPU timings. It supports model-based comparison, not a claim
to greater predictive accuracy than those simulators.

The complete evaluation is informative partly because it limits the
original portability narrative. Source-mean practical reversals are
absent. Two adaptations execute equivalently. The nominally SLO-gated
adaptation improves mean latency while losing throughput and sometimes
tail performance; its urgency gate is inactive. Window and metric changes
still produce practical reversals, so aggregate stability does not imply
uniform performance. A separate known-truth study shows why the earlier
inference pipeline cannot turn these descriptive comparisons into general
calibration claims.

We make four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An auditable execution, resource and information contract, with
explicit adaptation identities and independent event conformance checks.
\item A complete 5,760-cell evaluation over 120 workload windows, six
inherited arrival/deadline settings and two reservation capacities,
including absolute performance and matched-window trade-offs.
\item A descriptive account of ranking stability that retains exact ties,
structural equivalences, numerical near-ties and strict practical reversals
at distinct aggregation levels.
\item A scoped interpretation of historical comparisons, informed by a
134,000-study calibration diagnostic that separates implementation fidelity
from mathematical definitions and statistical validity.
\end{enumerate}

Three questions organize the paper. \textbf{RQ1:} How do the absolute
performances of four adaptations vary across workload windows, inherited
arrival/deadline settings and KV reservation capacities?
\textbf{RQ2:} How do workload variation, aggregation, metric selection,
ties and practical differences affect scheduler rankings?
\textbf{RQ3:} What do the historical comparison audit and known-truth
calibration establish about the reliability and limitations of these
comparisons? Sections~\ref{sec:performance}--\ref{sec:inference} answer them
in that order.
