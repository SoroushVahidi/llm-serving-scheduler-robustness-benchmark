\section{Introduction}
A scheduler comparison assumes that different policy labels denote different
ways to execute the same workload. That assumption can fail even when every
policy sees identical requests. A label may select a different prefill model
or resource constraint; conversely, distinct priority lists may induce the
same state transitions. Establishing the comparison object therefore precedes
interpreting its winners.

The historical LLM-Serving Scheduler Portability (LSSP) corpus illustrates
the first problem: its implementations did not uniformly enforce prefill
work and KV capacity. Reproducing their ranking arithmetic cannot separate
scheduling decisions from policy-dependent execution semantics. We instead
evaluate four adaptations under \cm, a common contract specifying online
information, legal actions, work advancement, batch timing, and resource
acquisition and release. The adaptations retain selected ideas from FCFS
serving, chunked prefill, decode-first scheduling, and SLAI-inspired prompt
ordering \citep{kwon2023vllm,agrawal2024sarathi,slai2025}.

Prior work already establishes the importance of workload choice
\citep{papaioannou2024workload}, fair evaluation settings
\citep{agrawal2025evaluation}, and user-relevant metrics
\citep{agrawal2024etalon}. Our question is more specific: \emph{which nominal
scheduler distinctions survive a declared execution contract, and what
performance comparisons remain after equivalent behaviors are identified?}
The contribution is an auditable connection between the contract's
state-transition consequences and the residual empirical ranking problem.
It has three parts:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Structural reduction.} A residency/work bound and common
batch-end timestamps make chunked-FCFS and decode-first equivalent, despite
possibly different event-span ordering. Clock updates keep the urgency gate
inactive; prompt-length admission and whole-prefill/decode exclusion remain
active distinctions. Four labels thus yield three observed behaviors.
\item \textbf{Measured residual trade-offs.} Complete matched outcomes show
how the surviving rules affect mean latency, tail latency, finite-window
throughput, and deadline attainment. A retrospectively selected saved-record
example explains their coexistence without treating it as a representative
window or a causal decomposition.
\item \textbf{Ranking interpretation.} Source-mean practical stability
coexists with window-level and metric-dependent reversals. A post hoc view
removing duplicate behaviors and endpoints checks that this contrast is not
an artifact of the nominal comparison count.
\end{enumerate}

The deliberately fixed batch cost removes composition-dependent service
costs, while bounded residency preserves admission competition. This makes
\cm\ useful for analyzing executable distinctions, rather than predicting
a particular GPU. The results neither introduce a scheduling principle nor
establish that prompt-length ordering is optimal. Their scope is this
contract and the complete selected corpus; completeness makes the findings
checkable, not general.

Three questions organize the evidence:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,labelindent=0pt]
\item[RQ1: Performance.] What trade-offs remain among the distinct behaviors
across workload windows, arrival/deadline settings, and residency capacities?
\item[RQ2: Rankings.] How do aggregation, metric choice, exact ties, and
practical margins change the resulting preferences?
\item[RQ3: Historical interpretation.] What separates reproducible historical
arithmetic from calibrated inference?
\end{description}
The first two questions constitute the scheduler comparison. STAT01 supports
the third through synthetic known-truth diagnostics; it supplies neither
additional C01 performance observations nor population guarantees for its
selected trace windows.
