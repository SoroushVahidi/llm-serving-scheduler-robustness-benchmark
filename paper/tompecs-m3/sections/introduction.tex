\section{Introduction}
Distinct scheduling priority lists can select the same work and induce the
same request-state transitions. Aggregate rankings cannot distinguish this
structural equivalence from coincidentally similar performance. Evaluation
therefore requires establishing what the execution model allows each policy
to change.

Workload sensitivity, metric dependence, and throughput--latency trade-offs
are already well established \citep{papaioannou2024workload,agrawal2024etalon,agrawal2024sarathi}.
Vidur and Vidur-Bench support shared simulation and workload/policy comparisons
\citep{vidur2024}; evaluation guidelines address baseline fairness and
misleading summaries \citep{agrawal2025evaluation}. Those contributions
motivate a more specific question: \emph{which nominal scheduler distinctions
survive a declared execution contract, why do others disappear, and what
performance trade-offs remain?} Answering it requires examining state
transitions and mechanism activation alongside performance measurements.

Our methodological approach is to derive effective scheduler behavior from
the contract before interpreting performance differences: establish
conditional equivalence where the assumptions permit it, determine whether
the implemented mechanisms can activate, and measure the trade-offs that
survive. The state-transition argument, inactivity certificate, and saved
execution checks below support distinct parts of this analysis; observed
agreement outside the proof's assumptions is not a general equivalence
theorem.

We investigate four project-maintained adaptations under \cm, a contract
specifying online information, legal actions, work advancement, batch timing,
and resource acquisition and release. The adaptations retain selected ideas
from FCFS serving, chunked prefill, decode-first scheduling, and SLAI-inspired
prompt ordering \citep{kwon2023vllm,agrawal2024sarathi,slai2025}.
Fixed batch cost removes composition-dependent service costs; four or eight
resident slots preserve admission competition. This abstraction permits a
precise account of executable distinctions, without predicting a particular
GPU or comparing native serving systems. The complete selected corpus has
120 frozen production-derived windows, six joint arrival/deadline settings,
and two residency capacities.

The scientific contribution connects three questions and their answers:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,labelindent=0pt]
\item[RQ1: Effective behavior.] Which policy distinctions change request
execution? A residency/work bound and common batch-end timestamps imply
identical per-request transitions for chunked-FCFS and decode-first under
the original contract. All 1,440 matched terminal comparisons confirm this
invariant; four labels yield three observed behaviors. A separate 72-cell
budget sensitivity preserves the pair's equality in its selected stratum,
an empirical finding beyond the original proof's conditions.
\item[RQ2: Mechanism inactivity.] Why does SLO-gated's urgency rule not
execute? Clock updates give zero anchor age in the original contract. A
finite-work bound also rules out first activation under the examined binding
budgets. Completion deadlines do not enter the gate. The measured SLO-gated
effects consequently cannot be attributed to urgency; prompt-length
admission ordering remains an active distinction.
\item[RQ3: Residual trade-offs and rankings.] What preferences remain, and
at what comparison level? Measured mean-latency improvements coexist with
throughput losses and, on BurstGPT, larger mean window-p95 latency.
Source-mean practical stability coexists with window-level and
metric-dependent reversals. Removing duplicate behaviors and endpoints
preserves that contrast, delimiting what an aggregate ranking can establish.
\end{description}

Section~\ref{sec:contract} gives the structural arguments;
Sections~\ref{sec:performance} and \ref{sec:rankings} quantify the surviving
comparisons. Event reconstruction and numerical verification support the
findings. The separate historical diagnostic in Section~\ref{sec:inference}
examines statistical calibration; it adds no common-contract observations.
The selected windows support finite-corpus conclusions. Transfer to other
execution contracts remains an open modeling question.
