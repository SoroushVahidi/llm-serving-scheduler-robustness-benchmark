\section{A Common Execution Contract}\label{sec:contract}
\subsection{Why the comparison object matters}
The historical corpus contains 9,360 configurations and two deterministic
verification passes per configuration. Its primary panel contains eleven
policies, with two additional style approximations. The historical engine
permits policy-dependent service modes: instantaneous prefill for some
policies, explicit prefill work for others, and differing enforcement of KV cache
limits. A policy's requested work can also differ from the engine's realized
progress. Consequently, those outcomes cannot be interpreted as a controlled
comparison of scheduling order. Table~\ref{tab:objects} distinguishes the
three evidence objects used in this paper.

The common contract distinguishes changes in the order of work that all
executes from changes in which requests enter or progress. Fixing batch cost
removes composition-dependent execution cost; bounded residency retains
admission competition. We do not claim prospective selection of these settings
for the equivalence or trade-offs reported here.

\subsection{Service, resources, and information}
\cm\ represents one abstract device. Each batch lasts 1\,ms and has a
32,768-token work budget. Each executed prompt token consumes one unit;
each selected decode request consumes one unit and emits one token.
All policies share these rules. A request emits no output during its own
prefill, including its final chunk; its decoding starts in a later batch.
Other resident requests may decode in the same batch. Batch duration
is independent of its composition. Thus seconds and tokens per second
below are model quantities, not estimates for a particular GPU or LLM.

Each resident request reserves the complete 32,768-token context bound.
All frozen request lengths fit; the maximum prompt-plus-output length is
30,053. Capacities of 131,072 and 262,144 tokens therefore permit
\emph{four and eight resident requests}, respectively, even though the
configured sequence limit is 64. Actual used KV follows executed prompt
and output prefixes, rounded to 16-token blocks, and must remain within
the reservation. Completion releases the reservation. Waiting requests own
none. This conservative rule avoids output-length prediction and allocation
deadlock; it does not model incremental native paging or cache eviction.

There is no preemption, dropping, migration, prefix sharing, or recomputation.
Requests arriving during a batch become eligible at its end. Idle time
advances to the next arrival. The administrative horizon is the final
arrival plus 50\,s; a batch cannot finish beyond it. Unfinished requests
remain explicitly unfinished rather than contributing observed completion
latencies.

Policies see only arrived request identifiers, stable order, arrival time,
prompt length, acknowledged progress, residency, last-output/prefill-completion
anchor, and current time. Actual remaining output length, future arrivals,
source identity, and output-derived metric deadlines are hidden. The engine
either executes the exact proposed token spans or rejects the plan. The
shared contract therefore fixes both the resources and the online
information available to each adaptation.

\subsection{Reachable-state equivalence}\label{sec:equivalence}
We distinguish four observations: candidate ordering; selected per-request
work; state transitions and timestamps; and terminal outcomes and metrics.
Equality at a later level alone does not imply equality at an earlier one.
A \emph{normalized journal} sorts the selected and executed spans by request
identifier, retaining their types, endpoints, and all other fields.

\paragraph{Assumptions and state.}
Consider a finite valid trace with positive integer prompt and output lengths
$p_i,o_i$, nondecreasing finite arrivals, and stable identifiers resolving
simultaneous arrivals. Each prompt satisfies $p_i\leq B$ and each total
length fits the reserved context. Both runs start empty and use one fixed
policy throughout, the same fixed positive integer budget $B$, chunk cap
$C=512$, unit decode work, and reservation capacity $K\geq1$. Admission
occurs only on first positive prefill work; slots are released after batch
completion. Residents precede waiting requests, admissions use a stable
arrival-order prefix, and resident prefills retain that order. There is no
preemption, bypass, or external admission. Exogenous arrivals during a batch
become eligible at the next boundary. Batch duration is the same fixed
positive value in both runs, with a finite, advancing clock and common horizon.

At a boundary, a request has prefilled progress $x_i$, generated progress
$y_i$, residency, an anchor, and first-output/completion timestamps. A
resident is unfinished and either prefilling or decode-ready. Its work
offer before batch-budget truncation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:offer}
 q_i=\begin{cases}\min(C,p_i-x_i),&x_i<p_i,\\1,&x_i=p_i.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
An executable span records request, phase, and progress endpoints; a request
appears at most once per batch. Chunked-FCFS visits residents in stable order,
then waiting requests. Decode-first visits resident decodes, resident
prefills, then waiting requests, preserving stable order within each group.
Both allocate the minimum of an offer in Equation~\ref{eq:offer} and the
remaining budget, subject to reservation capacity for new admissions.

\begin{lemma}[Resident-prefix slack]\label{lem:prefix}
At every reachable boundary, admissions have followed a stable arrival-order
prefix. For residents $R=(r_1,\ldots,r_m)$ in that order, if $m>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prefix}
 \sum_{j<m}q_{r_j}\leq B-1.
\end{equation}
If total resident demand exceeds $B$, the youngest resident $r_m$ is prefilling.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The empty initial state satisfies the claim. We give the conditional
selection and preservation steps together to avoid assuming the invariant
at an unproved successor. Under Equation~\ref{eq:prefix}, the earlier
residents' offers fit and leave at least one token for the last resident.
If total demand fits, both policies serve every offer fully. If it exceeds
$B$, the last resident cannot be a unit-cost decode; it is a prefill and
remains last under decode-first. Permuting the earlier residents therefore
leaves exactly the same residual allocation to it. Both policies next
admit the same waiting prefix with the same residual budget and slots.

Use the resulting chunked-FCFS execution list $Q$, ordered by stable arrival.
Each selected request receives an integer allocation $e_i\geq1$, with total
at most $B$. Only the final entry can receive less than its offer: a
truncated prefill exhausts the budget. A fully served survivor has next
offer $q_i'\leq e_i$: remaining prefill decreases, a completed prefill
becomes a unit-cost decode, and a continuing decode still costs one.
A truncated final prefill survives and is the youngest resident; no
admission follows it. If any resident survives, let $z$ be the youngest.
All other survivors were fully served, including when completions leave
holes in the resident order. Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prefix-preservation}
 \sum_{i\in R'\setminus\{z\}}q_i'
 \leq\sum_{i\in R'\setminus\{z\}}e_i
 \leq\sum_{i\in Q}e_i-e_z\leq B-1.
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:prefix-preservation} proves the successor inequality; if
$z$ were a decode, its unit offer would make total demand at most $B$.
Empty successor states are immediate. Prefix admission and completion
removal preserve arrival order; later arrivals cannot introduce an older
identifier. Idle jumps and new waiting arrivals change no resident offer.
Induction proves the claim, including simultaneous arrivals and partially
processed prefills.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-policy equivalence from an empty system]\label{thm:equivalence}
Under the assumptions above, chunked-FCFS and decode-first select identical
per-request executable spans at every boundary. They have identical
normalized journals, request states, timestamps, terminal outcomes, and
metrics, for every valid trace and every fixed admissible $B$ and $K$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Couple the runs at the empty state. The conditional selection argument in
Lemma~\ref{lem:prefix} gives the same spans at each coupled boundary.
Per-request updates commute and share one batch-end timestamp; reservation
sums and completion sets are order-independent. Thus successor states,
anchors, first outputs, and completions coincide. The lemma preserves the
induction hypothesis. Identical idle jumps, arrival eligibility, horizon
checks, and stopping decisions also couple any unfinished outcomes.
Applying the same metric definitions to identical terminal records proves
the final assertion. Ordered span arrays may still differ.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Binding example and scope.}
For simultaneous arrivals with prompts $(1024,1,1024)$, outputs $(3,3,3)$,
$B=1024$, and $K=4$, the first batch allocates prefills $(512,1,511)$.
The next offers are $(512,1,512)$, totaling $1025>B$: a later ready decode
coexists with an earlier unfinished prefill. Both policies allocate
$(512,1,511)$, moving only the decode's position in the span list.
Thus the theorem does not assume that all offers fit. It covers four/eight
residents and budgets 1,024/2,048 when inputs satisfy the unchanged domain.
It does not cover arbitrary injected states, policy switching, changing
budgets/chunk rules, multi-token decode, waiting-before-resident policies,
or asynchronous/preemptive systems. The invariant encloses reachable states;
it is not a necessary-and-sufficient characterization of every possible
state. Native serving systems are outside this theorem.

\paragraph{Nonbinding corollary and remaining mechanisms.}
In the original configuration, at most $K$ existing or newly admitted
requests each offer at most 512 tokens, so $512K\leq4096<32768$.
This simpler sufficient condition also proves equivalence; the theorem
explains why reducing the budget alone cannot separate the fixed pair.
All 1,440 matched terminal comparisons confirm its realization in the
selected corpus. The proof, rather than these finite checks, establishes
its scope over valid arrival histories.

The same work bound includes SLO-gated's residual decodes, even though they
follow waiting prefills. A newly decode-ready resident's anchor is its
prefill batch's end; every later decode resets it to the next batch end.
There is no idle jump while such a resident exists. At each scheduling
boundary its anchor age is therefore zero under the implemented clock
updates, far below 0.495\,s. The urgency gate never binds. Prompt-length
ordering of waiting admissions remains active; resident prefills still
progress, and later arrivals cannot preempt admitted requests. Whole-FCFS
also retains a distinct rule: admitting whole prefills defers all decodes
in that batch. Neither mechanism is removed by the work bound. Thus the model does more
than give policies identical settings: it identifies which of their decision
rules can change state transitions. The resulting finite-window completion
distributions are the empirical object of Section~\ref{sec:performance}.

\subsection{Urgency inactivity and its sufficient condition (RQ2)}\label{sec:urgency}
Urgency concerns a token gap, not a synthetic completion deadline. At a
planning boundary $t$, an arrived, unfinished resident is critical exactly
when its prefill is complete, its anchor $\alpha_i$ is defined, and
$t-\alpha_i\geq0.495$\,s. The anchor is the end of final prefill or the last
executed decode; an omitted decode does not reset it. Waiting requests and
unfinished prefills cannot qualify. Neither completion deadlines nor
estimated remaining service enters this predicate. The protocol interprets
0.495\,s as a uniform 0.5\,s token-gap allowance minus a 5\,ms margin, but
the implementation fixes the threshold: it exposes no SLO parameter.
Changing finite metric deadlines alone cannot change execution.

\paragraph{Finite-work inactivity certificate.}
A finite-work argument also limits activation when the budget binds. Let
$P$ be total prompt work, $B>K-1$ the integer budget, $K$ the residency
limit, and $\delta$ the fixed batch duration. Suppose a first critical
decision exists. In each consecutive batch omitting that ready decode
since its anchor, the residual loop must have exhausted $B$; at most
$K-1$ other decodes consume one token each. Every such batch therefore
executes at least $B-(K-1)$ prefill tokens. With no recomputation or idle
jump while the decode remains resident, $m$ omitted batches imply
\begin{equation}\label{eq:urgency-bound}
 m[B-(K-1)]\leq P,\qquad
 t-\alpha_i\leq\delta\left\lfloor\frac{P}{B-(K-1)}\right\rfloor.
\end{equation}
By Equation~\ref{eq:urgency-bound}, $\delta\lfloor P/[B-(K-1)]\rfloor<0.495$\,s rules out first activation:
the finite prompt work cannot sustain enough consecutive decode omissions
to reach the urgency threshold.
The common-domain restriction gives $P\leq NB$ for $N$ requests. For
$N=200$, $B\geq1,024$, $K\leq8$ and $\delta=1$\,ms, the bound is at most
201\,ms. This is a sufficient inactivity certificate for these assumptions,
not a general waiting-time guarantee or a necessary condition for activation.
The original nonbinding case has the tighter zero-age proof above.

The fixed-threshold code agrees with its declared adaptation; configurable
token-gap classes in the historical scheduler are a different implementation.
Engineering checks at the adjacent floating-point values below and above
0.495, and at equality, confirm the predicate without establishing that
such supplied states are reachable. The hidden metric deadline cannot exercise the gate; altering the policy
literal would instead change the comparison object.

\subsection{Conformance evidence}
The event referee reconstructs request states, token spans, timing,
completion, and resource use from the inputs and execution journal without
importing the engine or policy implementation. It checks that intended and
executed work agree and recomputes the metrics. Every campaign cell passed
this check; an independent analyzer also checked the complete ranking and
reversal reports. The final evidence review additionally checked all result
identities and terminal metrics and replayed 24 structurally selected new-cell
journals. Seven previously validated hand-derived fixtures cover mixed binding work,
completion holes, staggered arrivals, unit budget, and horizon censoring.
Checks of 36 saved budget-study journals examine 37,034 boundaries and agree
with Lemma~\ref{lem:prefix}; these finite checks support implementation
conformance, not the theorem's universal quantifier. The authoritative
engine and policies are unchanged. These checks establish conformance to
\cm, not GPU fidelity.
