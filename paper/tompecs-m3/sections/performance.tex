\section{Absolute Performance and Trade-offs (RQ1)}\label{sec:performance}
\subsection{Completion and performance magnitudes}
Every cell completes all 200 requests: 1,152,000 request executions across
repeated conditions, not independent requests. There is no horizon censoring
and no undefined cell-level latency, TTFT, or TPOT endpoint. TPOT populations
range from 103 to 200 because single-output requests are excluded from that
particular metric. Across cells, mean latency ranges from 0.008314 to
3.893923\,s, p95 latency from 0.008560 to 7.300052\,s, and token throughput
from 332.489 to 7,766.685 tokens per simulated second.

Table~\ref{tab:absolute} reports absolute source--capacity aggregates.
Chunked-FCFS and decode-first share a row because their terminal records
and all metrics are exactly equal; their separate identities remain in the
artifact. Reporting this equivalence avoids treating duplicated outcomes
as corroborating evidence from two different behaviors.

\subsection{Mean latency gains do not imply a universal improvement}
SLO-gated reduces aggregate mean latency against chunked-FCFS in all six
source--capacity groups, by 11.3--34.0\%. The largest aggregate reduction is
Azure at four slots, from 2.8132 to 1.8559\,s. At eight slots, BurstGPT changes
from 0.1907 to 0.1692\,s, the smallest percentage improvement. This is a
substantial difference in the model's mean response time, but it comes
with a throughput reduction in every group, ranging from 1.65\% to 4.01\%.

The tail comparison is less favorable. For BurstGPT, mean window-p95 latency
rises from 1.0005 to 1.0377\,s at four slots and from 0.3707 to 0.4410\,s at
eight slots: increases of 3.72\% and 18.97\%. Shorter average latency can
therefore coexist with longer upper-tail latency under the same contract.
At four-slot Bailian, \anwg\ is also 3.12\% lower for SLO-gated despite its
24.05\% mean-latency reduction. Throughput, deadline attainment, and response
time reward different aspects of the completed schedule.

Because the urgency gate does not bind (Section~\ref{sec:contract}), these
observations concern prompt-length admission ordering and the declared
chunking rules. Earlier admission of short prompts can change who waits
and which requests finish last. The measured mean/tail/throughput contrast
is consistent with that ordering distinction, but the study does not isolate
separate causal contributions by intervention or validate the original
SLAI system. Prompt length is observable prefill work, not known total
service time: the policy cannot see remaining output length.
For a matched, fully completed 200-request condition, mean latency depends
on $\sum_i(c_i-a_i)/200$, whereas token throughput is
$\sum_i o_i/(\max_i c_i-\min_i a_i)$. Reducing the sum of completion times
need not reduce the last completion time or an upper quantile. This explains
why those objectives can disagree without a change in output count or
batch cost. It does not assert that every window's drain grows: the reported
source/capacity throughput means average window rates, and are not the
inverse of a mean drain time. Deadline attainment is a third functional,
counting which individual completions cross synthetic deadlines; the Bailian
loss therefore does not contradict its mean-latency gain.

\subsection{Capacity and inherited-setting effects}
Increasing reservation capacity doubles permitted residency from four to
eight. In every source--adaptation aggregate shown, mean and mean-p95 latency
fall and throughput rises. For chunked-FCFS, Azure mean latency falls from
2.8132 to 1.3626\,s while throughput rises from 3,746.2 to 7,018.7 tokens/s;
BurstGPT changes from 0.5225 to 0.1907\,s and from 3,354.9 to 5,532.1 tokens/s.
The capacity axis is predominantly a concurrency constraint in this model.
The largest observed used KV is 47,872 tokens in the four-slot profile and
62,880 in the eight-slot profile, both below even the smaller nominal
capacity. These results do not measure native allocator pressure, eviction,
or the benefit of paging at an exhausted used-token limit.

Figure~\ref{fig:absolute} separates settings within each source--capacity.
The inherited setting labels can produce large changes in goodput with
small changes in latency or throughput. For Azure chunked-FCFS at four
slots, LOW to HIGH\_PRESSURE changes mean latency from 2.7858 to 2.8269\,s
and throughput from 3,745.9 to 3,746.3 tokens/s, while \anwg\ falls from
0.291375 to 0.112625. Since the transformation also contracts deadline slack,
this is not evidence for an isolated arrival-load effect on goodput.

\subsection{Matched-window variation}
Figure~\ref{fig:matched} displays all 40 window-level matched differences
per source and capacity, averaging the six paired setting differences
within each window. It complements the source means by showing where a
performance difference changes sign or is concentrated. The vertical
zero line represents equal performance; it is neither a confidence bound
nor the 10\% practical-reversal threshold. For latency, a negative difference
favors SLO-gated; for throughput, it favors chunked-FCFS.

At the unaveraged condition level, SLO-gated uniquely minimizes mean latency
in all 480 Azure conditions, 468 of 480 Bailian conditions, and only 198 of
480 BurstGPT conditions. Its favorable source aggregates therefore do not
mean that it wins every window/setting comparison. The tail and throughput
distributions make the same distinction visible without reducing each
comparison to a winner label.

\paragraph{Answer to RQ1.}
Absolute performance varies with workload and permitted residency, and
SLO-gated's aggregate mean-latency advantage coexists with throughput losses
and BurstGPT tail-latency losses. Completion is uniformly one. The capacity
contrast primarily reflects four versus eight full-context residents, and
the setting contrast jointly changes arrival and deadline stress. Two
nominal adaptations have identical behavior under the evaluated contract.
